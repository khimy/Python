Areal Analysis

Average a variable over an area for a period you choose, and compare the standard methods side by side.

Method options

Methodology

P = areal value, Pᵢ = value at station i, A = total area, n = number of stations. Station values are the period totals (precipitation, ET₀, radiation, sunshine) or period means (temperature, humidity, soil moisture, wind).

1. Arithmetic Mean

P = (P₁ + P₂ + … + Pₙ) / n

The simple average of the stations that lie inside the boundary. Every station has the same weight. It is fine for flat areas with evenly spaced stations and small differences between them, but is biased when stations are clustered (for example many stations on the coast).

2. Thiessen Polygons

P = Σ (Aᵢ · Pᵢ) / A

Each point in the area is given to its nearest station, which divides the area into polygons (perpendicular bisectors between neighbouring stations). Each station is weighted by the area of its polygon inside the boundary, Aᵢ / A. Stations just outside the boundary also count when their polygon reaches inside. It corrects for uneven station spacing but ignores terrain, and values jump at polygon edges.

Here the polygons are computed on the grid (each cell goes to its nearest station) and cell areas use the exact spherical area of each latitude/longitude cell.

3. Isohyetal Method

P = Σ [Aⱼ · (Iⱼ + Iⱼ₊₁) / 2] / A

Lines of equal value (isohyets) are drawn from the station values. The area Aⱼ between two neighbouring isohyets Iⱼ and Iⱼ₊₁ is multiplied by their average, and the results are summed. It is usually the most accurate classic method because it follows the real pattern of rainfall, including the effect of mountains, if the isohyets are drawn well.

Instead of drawing the lines by hand, the surface is interpolated (IDW, spline or kriging, see options) and contoured at the chosen interval. Because each band uses the midpoint of its two isohyets, a dry area in the lowest band is counted at half the interval.

4. Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW)

P(x) = Σ wᵢ Pᵢ / Σ wᵢ , wᵢ = 1 / dᵢᵖ

The value at each grid point is a weighted average of all stations; closer stations get more weight. With p = 2 this is the classic reciprocal distance squared method. The areal value is the area-weighted mean of the grid inside the boundary. A larger p gives more local, "bull's-eye" patterns.

5. Thin-Plate Spline

P(x) = a₀ + a₁x + a₂y + Σ wᵢ · φ(‖x − xᵢ‖), φ(r) = r² ln r

A smooth surface that passes through every station (λ = 0) while bending as little as possible. Setting a smoothing λ > 0 lets the surface miss stations slightly to reduce wiggles. It gives smooth, realistic patterns but can overshoot between stations; negative values are set to zero for variables that cannot be negative.

6. Ordinary Kriging

P(x₀) = Σ λᵢ Pᵢ , Σ λᵢ = 1

γ(h) = c₀ + c · (1 − e^(−3h/a))

A geostatistical method. First the semivariogram γ(h) — how different two stations are as a function of their distance h — is estimated from the data and fitted with an exponential model (nugget c₀, sill c₀ + c, range a). The weights λᵢ are then chosen to give the unbiased estimate with the smallest error variance. It accounts for clustering and spatial correlation, but needs enough stations for a reliable variogram.

The variogram is fitted automatically by weighted least squares on 12 distance classes; the fitted values are shown in the results.

Boundary mean

All the grid methods (Thiessen, Isohyetal, IDW, Spline, Kriging) give the area-weighted mean inside the selected boundary: P = Σ (Aₖ · Pₖ) / Σ Aₖ over the grid cells k inside it. Choose the national boundary or draw your own catchment / region on the map. Stations outside the boundary still help the interpolation near the edge; only the Arithmetic Mean uses stations inside the boundary alone.

Data sources

NCM station observations — daily values measured at the National Center for Meteorology's synoptic stations. NCM shares these with the World Meteorological Organization (WMO); they are read here from NOAA NCEI's Global Summary of the Day archive, which is free and public. Units are converted to metric and relative humidity is calculated from the daily mean temperature and dew point. Days the station sent no precipitation report are counted as 0 mm, as NOAA does. Rainfall is the weak point of this exchange: stations often report that it rained without sending the amount. By default such stations are left out of rainfall averages (their totals would be too low); you can include them in "Method options". Temperature, humidity, dew point, wind and pressure are reported much more completely. The NOAA archive currently ends on 24 August 2025.

ERA5 model data — ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis via the Open-Meteo Historical API, sampled at each station's coordinates. It has no gaps, covers 1940 to about 6 days ago, and includes variables stations do not report (ET₀, solar radiation, soil moisture), but it is a model estimate (about 25 km resolution), not a measurement.

Other sources — CHIRPS and IMERG satellite rainfall (via NASA SERVIR ClimateSERV), NASA POWER, and the GFS and ECMWF forecasts (choose a future period, up to 15–16 days ahead). Satellite sources give the value of the grid cell at each station, so the methods above work the same way. The "📚 All data sources" button in the sidebar lists every source and how it is used.

NCM's official, quality-controlled archive (including climatological and rain-gauge stations) is available on request through NCM's Meteorological Data Publishing or Utilization service; NCM asks for prior approval before its data is republished.
